Key findings
  • Only 2 of 29 variables correlate significantly with CGPA: satisfaction with academic life (r = .183, p = .021) and social-media time (r = −.173, p = .030).
  • The CGPA regression model is not significant overall — R² = .114, F(12,146) = 1.57, p = .106. Only social-media time (β = −.207, p = .025) and academic-life satisfaction (β = .202, p = .017) reach significance.
  • The well-being model is significant — R² = .244, F(10,148) = 4.77, p < .0001. Feeling overwhelmed (β = −.257, p = .001), sleep consistency (β = .202, p = .014) and CGPA (β = .181, p = .017) are the significant predictors.
  • Sleep variables cluster together: sleep hours, quality and consistency inter-correlate at r = .28–.37, forming a coherent sleep-health factor.
  • Screen time and social-media time are strongly linked (r = .42), and both travel with phone-checking during study (r = .41 / .29).
  • 82.5% of respondents rate their workload as moderate or heavier; 55% say "moderate".
  • Commute time is strongly right-skewed (skew = 1.62, SD = 34.6 min against a mean of 30.5) — a small tail of long commuters.
Method note: Range-style answers were recoded to midpoints; frequency words coded 1–5; Likert/ordinal items treated as approximately interval. The well-being variable is the analyst-constructed mean of Q31 (academic-life satisfaction) and Q32 (academic–personal balance) — it is not a validated scale.

Interpretation. Academic performance (CGPA) is poorly explained by the lifestyle and well-being variables collected here — the model accounts for only ~11% of variance and is not statistically significant overall. By contrast, the well-being composite is meaningfully predicted (≈24% of variance). The dominant driver is feeling overwhelmed by workload (β = −.257), followed by sleep-schedule consistency (β = .202) and CGPA itself (β = .181) — performance and well-being are mutually reinforcing. All VIFs are below 1.5, so multicollinearity is not a concern in either model.
Caution: the well-being outcome is a two-item composite constructed by the analyst (mean of academic-life satisfaction and balance satisfaction). Its relationships should be re-examined if a different well-being definition is used.
